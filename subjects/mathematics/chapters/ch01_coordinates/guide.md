# Chapter 1: Orienting Yourself: The Use of Coordinates

> 👨‍🏫 **Teacher's Overview:** Coordinate geometry bridges algebra and geometry! French philosopher René Descartes invented this system by connecting numbers to positions on a plane. In Class 9 CBSE, mastering the Cartesian coordinate system, understanding abscissa vs. ordinate, and knowing signs of coordinates across the four quadrants are essential for both math and physics motion graphs.

---

### 1. The Cartesian Coordinate System

To specify the position of any point on a plane surface, we need **two independent perpendicular reference lines**:
- **$x$-axis (Horizontal axis):** The horizontal number line $X'OX$. The equation of the $x$-axis is $y = 0$.
- **$y$-axis (Vertical axis):** The vertical number line $Y'OY$. The equation of the $y$-axis is $x = 0$.
- **Origin $O(0, 0)$:** The point where the two axes intersect perpendicularly. Its coordinates are $(0, 0)$.

---

### 2. Coordinates of a Point: Abscissa and Ordinate

Every point $P$ in the Cartesian plane is represented by an ordered pair $(x, y)$:
- **Abscissa ($x$-coordinate):** The perpendicular distance of the point from the $y$-axis measured along the $x$-axis.
- **Ordinate ($y$-coordinate):** The perpendicular distance of the point from the $x$-axis measured along the $y$-axis.

| Feature | Abscissa ($x$) | Ordinate ($y$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Reference Distance** | Perpendicular distance from $y$-axis | Perpendicular distance from $x$-axis |
| **Measurement Axis** | Measured along $x$-axis | Measured along $y$-axis |
| **Written Position** | First element: $(x, y)$ | Second element: $(x, y)$ |
| **Value on Axes** | $x = 0$ on the entire $y$-axis | $y = 0$ on the entire $x$-axis |

> ⚠️ **Exam Trap:** Remember that $(x, y) \neq (y, x)$ unless $x = y$! For example, point $(2, 5)$ is 5 units above the $x$-axis and 2 units right of the $y$-axis, whereas point $(5, 2)$ is 2 units above the $x$-axis and 5 units right of the $y$-axis.

---

### 3. Four Quadrants and Sign Conventions

The two axes divide the plane into four infinite regions called **quadrants**, numbered counter-clockwise:

| Quadrant | $x$-sign (Abscissa) | $y$-sign (Ordinate) | Coordinate Form | Example Point |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Quadrant I** | Positive ($x > 0$) | Positive ($y > 0$) | $(+, +)$ | $P(3, 4)$ |
| **Quadrant II** | Negative ($x < 0$) | Positive ($y > 0$) | $(-, +)$ | $Q(-2, 5)$ |
| **Quadrant III** | Negative ($x < 0$) | Negative ($y < 0$) | $(-, -)$ | $R(-4, -6)$ |
| **Quadrant IV** | Positive ($x > 0$) | Negative ($y < 0$) | $(+, -)$ | $S(5, -3)$ |

> 💡 **Points on Axes:** Points lying directly on the coordinate axes **do NOT belong to any quadrant**:
> - Point $(4, 0)$ lies on the **positive $x$-axis**.
> - Point $(-3, 0)$ lies on the **negative $x$-axis**.
> - Point $(0, 6)$ lies on the **positive $y$-axis**.
> - Point $(0, -5)$ lies on the **negative $y$-axis**.

---

### 4. Perpendicular Projections & Distance Along Grid Lines

- If perpendiculars $PL$ and $QM$ are drawn from points $P(x_1, y_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2)$ to the $x$-axis:
  - Foot of perpendicular $L$ has coordinates $(x_1, 0)$.
  - Foot of perpendicular $M$ has coordinates $(x_2, 0)$.
  - Distance $LM = |x_2 - x_1|$.
- Similarly, for perpendiculars to the $y$-axis at $N(0, y_1)$ and $K(0, y_2)$, distance $NK = |y_2 - y_1|$.
