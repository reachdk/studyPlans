## Chapter opening: why motion must be described carefully

Nature contains many kinds of motion, from astronomical objects and subatomic particles to animals, plants, dust, smoke, tides and clouds. Scientists make complex motion easier to study by first considering idealised, simpler forms. This chapter develops **linear motion**, or motion in a straight line, and **uniform circular motion**. It introduces displacement, average velocity and average acceleration and uses words, numbers, equations and graphs to describe motion.

## 4.1 Motion in a Straight Line

When an object moves along a straight line, its motion is called **linear motion** or **motion in a straight line**. Examples include a swimmer in a straight race lane, a vertically falling ball, a car on a straight highway and a train on a straight track. Describing such motion begins with identifying the object's position at different instants of time.

The figure shows familiar examples of objects moving along straight paths. Its instructional purpose is to connect the formal term *linear motion* with observable motion.

### 4.1.1 Describing position

An object's **position** at an instant is described by its distance and direction from a chosen fixed **reference point**. If the position relative to that reference point changes with time, the object is **in motion**. If it does not change with time, the object is **at rest**.

For one-dimensional motion, an origin `O` is placed on a straight line. The two possible directions are represented using positive and negative signs. By convention, positions to the right of `O` are generally positive and positions to the left are negative, although a different convention may be chosen and used consistently.

### Figures 4.2 and 4.3

- **Fig. 4.2 - An athlete running on a straight track:** introduces the moving object used in the next example.
- **Fig. 4.3 - Reference point and positions:** a line marked from `-20 m` to `100 m`, with origin `O` at `0 m`, point `B` at `40 m` and point `A` at `100 m`. It demonstrates how origin, distance and sign specify position.

### Definitions

- **Reference point:** a fixed point relative to which position is described.
- **Position:** the distance and direction of an object from a reference point at an instant.
- **Motion:** change in position relative to a reference point with time.
- **Rest:** no change in position relative to a reference point with time.

### Note: instant and interval

An **instant of time** is one clock reading at a particular moment. A **time interval** is the duration between two instants, or two clock readings.

### 4.1.2 Distance travelled and displacement

The athlete starts at `O` at `t = 0 s`, reaches `B` at `t = 4 s`, reaches `A` at `t = 10 s`, and returns to `B` at `t = 16 s`.

- Total distance travelled: `OA + AB = 100 m + 60 m = 160 m`.
- Final position relative to the start: `OB = 40 m` in the positive direction.
- Displacement: `40 m` in the positive direction.

**Displacement** is the net change in an object's position between two instants. It needs both magnitude and direction. The **magnitude** of displacement is the straight-line distance between the initial and final positions. Distance travelled needs only a numerical value and unit; it does not include direction. The SI unit of both distance and displacement is the metre (`m`).

The magnitude of displacement is always less than or equal to total distance travelled. For motion in a straight line, the two are equal only when the object moves in one direction without turning back.

### Definitions

- **Distance travelled:** total length of the path followed by an object.
- **Displacement:** net change in position between two instants, with magnitude and direction.
- **Magnitude:** the numerical value of a physical quantity together with its unit.

### 4.1.3 Average speed and average velocity

**Average speed** describes how fast or slow an object moves:

`average speed = total distance travelled / time interval` (Eq. 4.1)

Average speed has magnitude but no direction.

An object is in **uniform motion in a straight line** if it travels equal distances in equal intervals of time for every possible choice of equal intervals. Its speed is constant. If it travels unequal distances in equal intervals, it is in **non-uniform motion**; its speed may increase, decrease, or do both.

Speed alone does not describe direction. **Average velocity** gives both the rate at which position changes and the direction of that change:

`average velocity = change in position / time interval = displacement / time interval` (Eq. 4.2a)

Using symbols:

`v_av = s / t` (Eq. 4.2b)

For straight-line motion, the direction of velocity is the direction of displacement and is represented by a positive or negative sign. The SI unit of average speed and average velocity is metre per second (`m s^-1` or `m/s`); kilometre per hour (`km h^-1`) is also commonly used.

A **rate of change** is the ratio of a change in one quantity to the corresponding change in time. Average velocity is the average rate of change of position with time.

For straight-line motion in one direction, average speed equals the magnitude of average velocity.

### 4.1.4 Average acceleration

A sudden start or stop produces a jolt because velocity changes. **Average acceleration** measures how quickly velocity changes over a time interval:

`average acceleration = change in velocity / time interval` (Eq. 4.3a)

`average acceleration = (final velocity - initial velocity) / time interval` (Eq. 4.3b)

If velocity changes from `u` at `t_1` to `v` at `t_2`:

`a = (v - u) / (t_2 - t_1)` (Eq. 4.3c)

The SI unit is `m s^-2` or `m/s^2`. Acceleration has magnitude and direction. In straight-line motion, acceleration is in the direction of velocity when speed increases and opposite to velocity when speed decreases. Acceleration may result from a change in the magnitude of velocity, its direction, or both.

An object can move very fast and still have zero acceleration if its velocity is constant. Acceleration concerns change in velocity, not how large the velocity already is.

A bus increases velocity from `36 km h^-1 (10 m s^-1)` to `54 km h^-1 (15 m s^-1)` in `10 s`, giving:

## 4.2 Graphical Representation of Motion

Graphs show visually how position, velocity and acceleration change with time. They help compare objects, calculate physical quantities and identify uniform or non-uniform motion. In this chapter, line graphs represent one quantity, such as position or velocity, against time.

All graphs in this chapter concern motion in a straight line in one direction. In this special case:

- distance travelled equals magnitude of displacement;
- speed equals magnitude of velocity;
- if position is zero at time zero, a position-time graph is also a distance-time graph;
- under the same condition, a velocity-time graph is also a speed-time graph.

### 4.2.2 Position-time graphs

A **position-time graph** shows change in position with time.

- A straight sloping line indicates constant velocity because equal displacements occur in equal time intervals.
- A curved line indicates changing velocity and therefore accelerated motion.
- A horizontal line indicates that position does not change: the object is at rest.
- The steeper of two straight position-time lines represents the greater magnitude of velocity.

The slope of a position-time graph is the rate of change of position with time. Between points `A(s_1,t_1)` and `B(s_2,t_2)`:

`average velocity = (s_2 - s_1) / (t_2 - t_1) = BC / CA`

The slope describes the line's steepness. More generally, graph slope gives the rate of change of the Y-axis quantity with respect to the X-axis quantity.

- **4.13(a):** a straight position-time line; equal `20 m` displacements in selected equal intervals; constant velocity.
- **4.13(b):** a curved line; increasing displacement in equal intervals; increasing velocity.

## 4.3 Kinematic Equations for Motion in a Straight Line with Constant Acceleration

For constant acceleration, acceleration at every instant equals average acceleration over any interval. If initial velocity is `u` at `t = 0`, final velocity is `v` at time `t`, acceleration is `a`, and displacement is `s`, the chapter derives three equations.

From `a = (v-u)/t`:

`v = u + at` (Eq. 4.4a)

From the area under the velocity-time graph:

`s = ut + (1/2)at^2` (Eq. 4.4b)

Eliminating `t` between the first two equations:

`v^2 = u^2 + 2as` (Eq. 4.4c)

These **kinematic equations** relate five quantities: displacement `s`, time interval `t`, initial velocity `u`, final velocity `v` and acceleration `a`. They mathematically describe and predict motion, but are valid only when acceleration is constant.

For one-direction straight-line motion, distance equals displacement magnitude and speed equals velocity magnitude. If motion can occur in both directions, the signs of `u`, `v`, `a` and `s` must follow the chosen direction convention.

### Figure 4.19: Velocity-time graph with non-zero initial velocity

The graph begins at velocity `u` and ends at `v` after time `t`. Its slope is acceleration. The area under the graph is divided into a rectangle and triangle to derive Eq. 4.4(b).

### Example 4.8: Braking distance

A car brakes with acceleration `-4 m s^-2` until `v = 0`.

- From `u = 54 km h^-1 = 15 m s^-1`, Eq. 4.4(c) gives `s = 28.1 m`.
- From `u = 108 km h^-1 = 30 m s^-1`, it gives `s = 112.5 m`.

Doubling the initial speed makes the calculated braking distance four times as large under the same constant braking acceleration.

### Bridging Science and Society: safe stopping distance

After brakes are applied, a vehicle still moves before stopping. Stopping distance depends on initial velocity, road condition, braking capacity and driver reaction time. Safe following distance must therefore be adjusted to speed and conditions. The chapter also mentions vehicle-to-vehicle communication technology being developed to exchange warning signals and reduce collision risk.

## 4.4 Motion in a Plane

**Motion in a plane** is motion in two dimensions. Examples include one vehicle overtaking another, a kicked ball and a satellite moving in a circular path.

### Figure 4.21: Motion in a plane

The examples illustrate paths that cannot be fully described using only one straight-line coordinate.

### 4.4.1 Uniform circular motion

When an object moves along a circular path, it is in **circular motion**.

For a child moving on a merry-go-round from `A` through `B` to `C`:

- distance travelled is the curved path `ABC`;
- displacement is the straight line `AC`.

For one complete revolution of radius `R`:

- distance travelled is the circumference `2 pi R`;
- displacement is zero because the final and initial positions coincide;
- if the revolution takes time `T`, average speed is `v_av = 2 pi R / T` (Eq. 4.5);
- average velocity over the revolution is zero.

When an object moves in a circular path with constant speed, its motion is **uniform circular motion**. Speed is constant, but the direction of velocity changes continuously. Therefore velocity changes and acceleration is non-zero even though speed does not change.

### Figure 4.22: Merry-go-round from above

The circular arc `ABC` represents distance, while chord `AC` represents displacement.

### Figure 4.23(a-c): Direction changes on closed tracks

An athlete runs at uniform speed around a rectangle, then a hexagon, then a circle. Direction changes four times on the rectangle, six times on the hexagon and continuously on the circle as the number of sides tends toward infinity. Velocity arrows show the changing direction.

### Ready to Go Beyond: three-dimensional motion

Motion through space, such as a car climbing a mountain road, a bird flying or an aircraft moving through air, is motion in three dimensions. Formal study is deferred to higher grades.

## Core Concept Checklist

- [ ] Position requires a reference point, distance, direction and an instant of time.
- [ ] Motion and rest depend on whether position relative to a reference point changes with time.
- [ ] Linear motion is motion along a straight line with a consistent sign convention.
- [ ] Distance travelled and displacement differ in path dependence, direction and possible values.
- [ ] Average speed and average velocity use distance and displacement respectively.
- [ ] Uniform and non-uniform motion are distinguished using distances covered in equal time intervals.
- [ ] Average acceleration is change in velocity per time interval and has magnitude and direction.
- [ ] Acceleration can result from a change in speed, direction or both.
- [ ] Motion data are plotted using labeled axes, units and suitable scales.
- [ ] Position-time graph shape and slope describe rest, constant velocity and changing velocity.
- [ ] Velocity-time graph shape and slope describe velocity and acceleration.
- [ ] Area under a velocity-time graph gives displacement for the chapter's stated motion cases.
- [ ] The three kinematic equations apply only to straight-line motion with constant acceleration.
- [ ] Braking distance connects initial velocity, braking acceleration, road conditions and reaction time.
- [ ] Motion in a plane is two-dimensional motion.
- [ ] Uniform circular motion has constant speed but continuously changing velocity direction.
- [ ] One circular revolution has distance `2 pi R`, zero displacement, average speed `2 pi R/T` and zero average velocity.
- [ ] Velocity on a circular path is tangent to the path at that point.

## At a Glance

- Position is specified by distance and direction from a reference point at an instant.
- Motion is a change of position relative to a reference point with time.
- Displacement is net change in position between two instants.
- Average speed is total distance divided by the time interval.
- Average velocity is displacement divided by the time interval.
- Average acceleration is change in velocity divided by the time interval.
- For constant acceleration in a straight line: `v = u + at`, `s = ut + (1/2)at^2`, and `v^2 = u^2 + 2as`.
- Uniform circular motion is motion along a circular path at constant speed; velocity direction changes continuously.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

| Misconception | Correction grounded in the chapter |
|---|---|
| Motion and rest are absolute. | Both are described relative to a chosen reference point. |
| Position needs only distance from the origin. | Direction, represented by a sign in one dimension, is also required. |
| Distance and displacement are interchangeable. | Distance is total path length; displacement is net change in position with direction. |
| Displacement can exceed distance travelled. | Its magnitude is always less than or equal to distance travelled. |
| A return trip has zero distance because displacement is zero. | Distance records the path and can be non-zero even when displacement is zero. |
| Speed and velocity are the same. | Speed has magnitude only; velocity also has direction. |
| Average speed is the simple average of two speed readings. | It is total distance divided by total time. |
| Negative acceleration always means an object is slowing down. | The sign shows direction relative to the chosen positive direction; slowing occurs when acceleration opposes velocity. |
| A fast object must have large acceleration. | Constant velocity gives zero acceleration, however large the velocity. |
| Constant speed means zero acceleration. | In uniform circular motion, direction changes continuously, so velocity changes and acceleration is non-zero. |
| A graph is a picture of the route. | A motion graph shows how one physical quantity changes with another. |
| A steeper position-time line means greater acceleration. | Its slope gives velocity; changing slope indicates acceleration. |
| The height of a velocity-time graph gives displacement. | Height gives velocity; area under the graph gives displacement. |
| Kinematic equations apply to every motion. | The equations in this chapter require constant acceleration and a consistent sign convention. |

## Authoritative enrichment (clearly separate from NCERT core)

The following is limited enrichment, not additional examinable NCERT content.

1. **Graph units as a self-check:** the slope of a position-time graph has units `m/s`, so it represents velocity; the slope of a velocity-time graph has units `(m/s)/s = m/s^2`, so it represents acceleration; area under a velocity-time graph has units `(m/s) x s = m`, so it represents displacement.
2. **Direction check for acceleration:** instead of treating a negative sign as automatically meaning “slowing,” compare the signs of velocity and acceleration. Same signs mean speed increases; opposite signs mean speed decreases for straight-line motion.

## Compact assessment blueprint

| Domain | Weight | Evidence expected | Suggested item types |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Position, reference point, distance and displacement | 18% | Defines and distinguishes quantities; applies direction and sign | MCQ, short response, route calculation |
| Average speed, velocity and acceleration | 22% | Selects correct quantity, unit and formula; interprets direction | Numerical, explanation, misconception check |
| Motion graphs | 25% | Plots data; reads position/velocity; uses slope and area | Graph construction, graph interpretation, calculation |
| Constant-acceleration equations | 20% | Chooses and applies a valid kinematic equation with consistent signs | Multi-step numerical, braking scenario |
| Motion in a plane and uniform circular motion | 10% | Explains constant speed with changing velocity; computes one-revolution quantities | Concept response, diagram-based item |
| Scientific practice and safety application | 5% | Connects evidence, assumptions and motion models to real situations | Activity analysis, road-safety application |

### Minimum balanced assessment

- 4 concept-selection items, including at least two misconception checks.
- 3 short explanations using NCERT terminology.
- 3 numerical problems: one average-rate problem, one graph-based problem and one kinematic-equation problem.
- 1 graph plotting task and 1 graph interpretation task.
- 1 activity or diagram-based question.
- 1 application question on braking distance or uniform circular motion.

### Quality criteria

- Every numerical item states units and enough information to solve it.
- Direction conventions are explicit wherever signs matter.
- Graph axes, units and scales are labeled.
- Items distinguish distance from displacement and speed from velocity.
- Kinematic equations are used only under constant acceleration.
- Any enrichment is labeled and is not required to answer NCERT-core items.
